Counties where childcare shortages and housing costs hit at once

CAREMap scores every U.S. county on childcare supply and on housing pressure for both families and childcare workers, then marks the places where both are severe. Those are the places where housing designed for home-based childcare providers could do the most good.

How the scores work

Childcare score: children under 5 per childcare worker (paid staff from County Business Patterns plus home-based solo providers from Nonemployer Statistics), smoothed for small counties, as a percentile within the county's group. Where the Census withholds a county's business count (0 to 2 businesses), it is scored under both 0 and 2 and the two are averaged.

Housing score: half families (share of renters paying 30% or more of income; median home value to median income) and half providers (2-bedroom rent as a share of a childcare worker's pay), as a percentile within the group.

Priority: High means both scores are in the group's top 25% whichever withheld-count assumption is used. Medium means the combined score (the geometric mean of the two, so a county must be high on both) is in the top 25%. Counties with fewer than 100 children under 5 are not scored.

What it can't tell you

Scores show where combined need is greatest, not where an intervention is proven to work. Childcare supply is measured in workers, not licensed slots, and is least reliable in rural counties whose business counts are withheld. County averages hide neighborhoods: Wayne County is one value here. College counties are flagged because student incomes inflate rent burden.

Data: Census ACS 2020–2024 5-year, County Business Patterns 2023, Nonemployer Statistics 2023, Vintage 2025 population estimates, 2024 county boundaries; HUD FY2027 Fair Market Rents; BLS OEWS May 2025; USDA 2023 Rural-Urban Continuum Codes; Michigan MiLEAP child care licensing.
