Counties where childcare shortages and housing costs hit at once

CAREMap scores every U.S. county on childcare supply and on housing pressure for both families and childcare workers, then marks the places where both are severe and checks how well an Ouray-style project would fit each one. It grew out of one street in Ouray, Colorado, where affordable homes were built so childcare providers could live and work in them. The question here is where else that idea could help.

It started on one street in Ouray, Colorado

Ouray County is a small mountain county near Telluride. When the project began, it had 158 children under 5 and 55 childcare slots, and in a 2022 survey four in five families said their childcare didn't meet their needs. One family drove nearly three hours a day to reach daycare in Montrose.

Rural Homes, a nonprofit housing developer, and Bright Futures, the region's early childhood council, decided to treat the housing shortage and the childcare shortage as one problem.

“Well, can we solve both of them at the same time?”Paul Major, founder of Rural Homes, to KUNC

Step 1 · Build it ready

Homes pre-licensed for childcare

The two homes were designed to meet state rules before anyone moved in: fencing, required square footage and a ramp so cribs can be carried out in an emergency. They still work as ordinary houses.

Step 2 · Train providers

A free business course

Bright Futures ran an evening course on starting a home childcare business. Six people began, three finished, and two were chosen after presenting business plans.

Step 3 · Make it affordable

A lender buys, the provider rents

Mission Driven Finance, a nonprofit lender, bought the two homes for $479,000 each and rents them to the providers below market, with an option to buy.

Step 4 · Open

Two home daycares on one street

Ouray Family Childcare opened in March 2025 and Tiny Colorado Treasures that spring. Each started with four children and can grow to 12, enough to quadruple the county's licensed home-based slots.

It isn't a finished fix

Infant spots at one home have a waitlist of about three years. Rent rises every year, and one provider has pushed her plan to buy from two years to about four. Because the home is tied to the business, a provider whose business struggles could also lose her housing. Reporting on the project notes that the area's shortage still isn't solved.

“Owning a home is within our reach now.”Melissa McChristian, owner of Ouray Family Childcare, to the Ouray County Plaindealer

Rural Homes has similar developments with possible pre-licensed childcare underway in Louisville, Longmont and Grand Junction, and Steamboat Springs has shown interest. Ouray found its need through a local survey. CAREMap asks where else in the country the same two problems meet.

Why look at childcare and housing together

Each shortage makes the other worse. CAREMap measures both because a fix for one alone can leave the other in place.

The two shortages don't travel together. Across counties, childcare and housing scores are slightly opposed, and a county's childcare score has nothing to do with its neighbors' housing costs. Each shortage clusters on its own, so the overlap happens where two separate patterns cross. One problem can't stand in for the other; both have to be measured.

In those places, the hardest part to copy would be recruiting and training providers, the job Ouray's early childhood council did, more than building the homes.

Three counties up close

Each was picked to test the map from a different angle, then checked against local reporting and county plans.

Maui County, HI

Metro #2 · High · Fit: Weak

Not a wildfire artifact.

The childcare figures come from March 2023, before the Lahaina fire, and a University of Hawai‘i count from 2022 agrees: 0.28 licensed seats per young child. Even with Maui's rent set back to its pre-fire level, Maui stays High (#3). The fire made both shortages worse afterward. But with population falling and the rebuild competing for land, builders and money, now is a poor time for an Ouray-style project.

Archuleta County, CO

Rural #3 · High · Fit: Possible

The closest match to Ouray.

Pagosa Springs has 6 licensed infant spots for about 100 births a year, and its 2025 housing study calls for at least 1,316 new homes by 2035, with short-term rentals a big part of the squeeze. It already has an early childhood council and town and county funding for childcare. As in Ouray, the hard part would be finding providers: it has very few licensed home daycares.

Lake County, MI

Rural #56 · Medium · #1 in Michigan · Fit: Weak

The overlap is real; the model would need adapting.

About two-thirds of Lake County's homes sit empty as seasonal cabins while year-round workers can't find housing, and 73% of its young children live in census tracts with no licensed provider or 3+ kids per slot. The county's housing plan already calls for modular homes and uses a land bank. But a typical home is about $134,000, so a local version would mean low-cost homes held as rentals for licensed providers, not new homes for sale.

How the scores work

Childcare score: children under 5 per childcare worker (paid staff from County Business Patterns plus home-based solo providers from Nonemployer Statistics), smoothed for small counties, as a percentile within the county's group. Where the Census withholds a county's business count (0 to 2 businesses), it is scored as 0, the assumption that best matched Michigan's licensed slots, and checked again at 2.

Housing score: half families (share of renters paying 30% or more of income; median home value to median income) and half providers (2-bedroom rent as a share of a childcare worker's pay), as a percentile within the group.

Priority: High means both scores are in the group's top 25%. A High county that would drop out if its withheld count were 2 instead of 0 is shown as High, unconfirmed (striped). Medium means the combined score (the geometric mean of the two, so a county must be high on both) is in the top 25%. Counties with fewer than 100 children under 5 are not scored.

Fit is a separate layer for High and Medium counties and never changes the ranking. It counts four conditions the Ouray project relied on: population holding or growing; a typical home worth at least $275,000, so new homes at Rural Homes' prices would sell below the market; a local pool of home-based providers; and a state license that lets a home grow to 12 children (2017 federal data). Strong = all four, Possible = three. Land, local approval, funding, a long-term owner and an early-childhood partner matter just as much but can only be checked locally. A fifth test, tight rental vacancy, was dropped because it failed Ouray County itself.

What it can't tell you

Scores show where combined need is greatest, not where an intervention is proven to work. Childcare supply is measured in workers, not licensed slots, and is least reliable in rural counties whose business counts are withheld. Fit uses a few national measures and can't see land, partners or local politics. County averages hide neighborhoods: Wayne County is one value here. College counties are flagged because student incomes inflate rent burden.

About CAREMap

CAREMap began after a Marketplace story about the Ouray homes raised a simple question: if housing built for childcare providers works there, where else could it help? It's an independent student project, not affiliated with Rural Homes or any organization named here.

Code, data and notebooks: github.com/rhogerheide/caremap
